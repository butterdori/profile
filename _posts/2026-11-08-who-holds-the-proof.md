*Daein Kang and Brian An · Georgia Institute of Technology · Presented at APPAM 2026*

When a disaster damages a home, federal assistance often depends on proving that you lived there. But what happens when the records are missing, destroyed, or held by someone else? Homeowners may be able to rely on property records, while renters may need a lease, rent receipt, or statement from a landlord who is also dealing with the disaster.

In **“Who Holds the Proof? Housing Tenure, Documentation, and Administrative Burden in Federal Disaster Assistance,”** we analyze **22.1 million FEMA Individuals and Households Program applications (2002–2025)** to understand whether renters and homeowners face different obstacles when documenting their claims.

We construct a study examining three outcomes in FEMA disaster assistance: **application approval, awards amount among approved applicants, and recorded reasons for denial**. Using FEMA's denial classifications, we distinguish **documentation-related denials** (additional documents required) from **substantive denials** (ineligible damages or needs, or existing insurance) to identify where disparities between renters and homeowners emerge. We also examine how household income, proximity to Disaster Recovery Centers, and prior local disaster experience are associated with these outcomes. We control for ACS socioeconomic indicators at the ZCTA-level.

## Main findings

- **Renters face a distinct documentation barrier.** In adjusted models, documentation-related denial is **2.42 percentage points higher for renters** (an implied **4.74% versus 2.32%** for homeowners), even as renters are **16.09 points less likely** to be denied on substantive grounds.
- **The gap persists over time.** The renter–homeowner documentation gap is positive every year from **2015 through 2023**, reaching **9.97 points in 2020** before narrowing in 2021–2023. The narrowing coincides with expanded FEMA verification options, whether the reforms have caused the change requires further investigation.
- **Income does not resolve the problem.** Documentation denials do not steadily decrease with household income. The clearest advantages appear only in the highest income brackets; part of the apparent income gradient reflects differences in tenure.
- Proximity to Disaster Recovery Centers and recent county disaster experience are also associated with higher approval, though those relationships vary by year.

We find that **administrative burden is not only about applicants' resources; it is also about who controls the evidence an agency requires.** When compliance depends on records held by third parties, even well-resourced applicants may face barriers beyond their control. Designing verification procedures that reduce reliance on third-party cooperation, including accepting alternative forms of evidence and enabling agencies to obtain records directly, would alleviate pressure on renters, a group that is already socially vulnerable before, during, and after disasters.
